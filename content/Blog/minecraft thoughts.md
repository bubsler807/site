"Do anything you want." This is the way that Minecraft is marketed. This was its promise, and in its earliest incarnations, Minecraft came close to delivering on that promise.

What exactly is there to do? You could build. You could get really into redstone, or command blocks, start building adventure maps ala hypixel, but at that point, why not just play modded, or another game entirely. It's hard to do what you want when it feels like **every entity in the world is put there for a singular purpose**, which is to propel you into "progressing".

Here is the problem with that:

> I think it makes a lot of sense that the remaining MUD players are those that have the most complete affinity for the system. While MUDs draw a technical distinction between making and playing, I think psychologically the lines are very blurry, because part of playing _is_ making.
> 
> One reason Minecraft really took off is that embedded in the choice to "live" in an expansive virtual world is a desire to affect that world. It mirrors the malleability of our world but on a larger scale. Climbing up an RPG power curve is only a pseudo-satisfier of that desire; you're not actually changing the world, just changing your position in it. Actually being able to modify the world you occupy is the base desire. In that respect, Minecraft is really the spiritual successor to MUDs. - from npunt on hackernews

These progression mechanics undermine the spirit of freedom, turning player agency into a disguised to-do list. It redefines the terms of engagement: your actions are now only valuable if they serve a larger, systemic function.

There are too many non-diegetic elements.

There is pretty much no variety within each biomes, the variety mostly coming from the biomes adjacent to the given one. Thus, when we try to find a place to build a base, what we are actually doing is looking for a plains biome that isn't surrounded by dark forest bullshit. It becomes a game of avoidance. The lack of variety in fact leads to a severely lacking sense of "place". There is only space.[^1] hundreds and hundreds of blocks of the same shit you've seen 1000 times before. Alas, we cannot experience the discovery of the same thing twice. In the old world-generation of alpha and beta, you were never far from some unique geological anomaly. Fixing the generation wouldn't fix the game, but **the very fact that it is this much of a problem** serves as a good illustration of the direction of the game as a whole. The emptiness of the world would be of no matter for the creative iconographic mind (As an example, I still have vivid memories of the constructions I witnessed being created on the flat plains of Gmod).

You can pretty much overcome through a few different ways: You could have a massive amount of systems facilitating novel, emergent scenarios (see dwarf fortress). Or something similar such as my idea of a game where players set up npcs that become their own entities in the world. if Mojang had gone this direction, it would have lost the quality of the world being untouched when you first arrive.

You would be merely a participant in the already ongoing simulation. Notch was heavily inspired by Dwarf Fortress (which has extremely complex social and ecological systems) in the beginning, but **made the choice not to have a pre-existing ecosystem**. Back in earlier versions, the world felt **ambivalent** to you being in it. You were merely a wanderer travelling through a familiar, yet somewhat alien world. You could choose to build, or to overcome some self-set challenge, but that was your **choice** (now it hardly feels like a choice). The world was not waiting for you to complete it, it simply existed.

Of course, this all mirrors a broader trend in game design where "content" is measured numerically by how much there is to do. I find playing Minecraft depressing now, because in a game that once thrived on _not_ telling you what to do, it feels particularly tragic.

I remember the update that added the requirement to eat (same update that added endermen actually). It was not well received. That was really the first thing that forced you to do something. Now you needed to constantly be farming and eating in order not to die, but previously food just restored hearts, which you could avoid losing by not being attacked.

Another thing that comes to mind is that things were immediately available to the player. Getting diamond could be done in literally a few minutes if you rush stone tools and dig straight down.

They keep adding things where seemingly the primary purpose is to be annoying.

In my opinion they should have just kept doing what they were doing earlier in development when they were adding potions, pistons, dispensers, trapdoors, wolves, etc. When they first added thunderstorms, there were plans to do a whole lot of stuff with the weather. Now it's only function is to be annoying and occasionally create charged creepers. They should have continued adding the ability for players themselves to create systems which interact with each other, or not.

## What I Like:

The fact that the world consists of low resolution cubes allows for a certain iconographic mentality to return. Similar to pixel art. "Realistic" graphics almost always lose sight of the creative aspects.
